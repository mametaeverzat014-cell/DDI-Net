# DEFENSE_GUIDE.md

Plain-language walkthrough of the study, section by section. For each part:
**what we did**, **why we did it**, **what the result means**, and — the part
that matters most in front of judges — **what it does not mean**.

Rule for the whole guide: if you cannot say what a result does *not* show, you do
not yet understand it well enough to defend it.

---

## The one-paragraph version

Drug interaction models are usually tested by splitting *pairs* of drugs at
random. That lets the same drug show up in both training and testing, so a model
can score well by recognising familiar drugs — which tells you nothing about a
brand-new drug. We split by *drug* instead, so every test drug was completely
unseen. Then we asked whether telling the model what each drug does biologically
(which proteins it hits, which pathways those proteins are in) helps it
generalise. It did — but the interesting part is that we then tried hard to prove
ourselves wrong, because well-studied drugs have more annotations, and a model
could look like it understands biology when it is really just detecting fame.

---

## 1. The research question

**What we did.** We asked one question: can biological information about a drug
substitute for the missing "who does this drug already interact with" context,
when the drug is new to the model?

**Why.** Because that is the situation that matters. A drug approved last month
has zero documented interactions. A model that needs those interactions to work
is useless exactly when you most need it.

**What it means.** The paper is organised around this question, not around
getting a high score.

**What it does not mean.** We are not claiming to have solved DDI prediction, and
we are not claiming our model is the most accurate one available. Several
published models are stronger at the molecular modelling part.

---

## 2. The dataset

**What we did.** Used 1,705 drugs and 191,392 documented interaction pairs from
Therapeutics Data Commons, which redistributes DrugBank.

**Why.** It is a standard, citable, openly available benchmark with a documented
version.

**What it means.** Results are reproducible from a named data version.

**What it does not mean.** These 1,705 drugs are *not* a representative sample of
DrugBank, which has roughly 15,000 drugs. Someone else chose this subset by
criteria we do not know. Also — and this trips people up — the source contains
**only** documented interactions. There are no negative examples anywhere in it.
Every "these two drugs do not interact" example in this study was generated by
us, which makes how we generated them a scientific decision, not a preprocessing
detail.

---

## 3. The split

**What we did.** Partitioned *drugs* into 1,195 training / 255 validation / 255
test. Then reported separately on S3: the pairs where **both** drugs are test
drugs.

**Why.** If you split pairs, aspirin can be in 400 training pairs and 100 test
pairs, and the model has effectively seen the answer. Splitting drugs makes that
impossible.

**What it means.** Every number in this paper is on drugs the model never saw.
Our scores are lower than many published DDI numbers, and that is the point — we
are answering a harder question.

**What it does not mean.** Drug-disjoint is not the hardest possible test. A test
drug can still share a chemical scaffold with a training drug. We implemented
scaffold-disjoint splitting but **did not run that evaluation**, so we do not
claim it.

---

## 4. Negative sampling

**What we did.** Generated negatives by picking drug pairs in proportion to how
many interactions each drug has ("degree-matched"), using degree counted from
training pairs only.

**Why.** If you pick negatives uniformly at random, hub drugs are common among
positives but rare among negatives. A model can then score well by asking "are
both of these drugs promiscuous?" without looking at chemistry at all. Degree
matching removes that shortcut.

**What it means.** The task is harder and our numbers are lower than they would
otherwise be. That is intentional.

**What it does not mean.** It does not mean our negatives are true negatives.
About 86.8% of the pair space is *unlabelled*, not negative, so some of our
negatives are almost certainly real interactions nobody has documented. This
pushes our reported performance down by an amount we cannot measure.

---

## 5. The model

**What we did.** Three encoders per drug — a graph network over the molecule, and
two Deep Sets encoders over the drug's protein set and pathway set — fused into
one drug vector, with a symmetric decoder for the pair.

**Why Deep Sets.** A drug's annotations are an unordered collection of varying
size (median 19 proteins, max 627). Deep Sets is the standard architecture for
that. We deliberately avoided a graph network over the biological graph, because
its usual sum aggregation is neighbour-counting — the very shortcut we were
trying to exclude.

**Why mean, not sum.** Sum over a set is a count in disguise. Mean normalises by
set size, so a drug with 4 annotations and one with 400 differ in *content*, not
magnitude. We kept sum as a control specifically to check whether counting was
what mattered.

**Why symmetric.** "A interacts with B" is the same statement as "B interacts
with A". We enforce this exactly using only commutative operations, rather than
hoping the model learns it.

**What it means.** The architecture is designed so that the shortcut hypothesis
is testable rather than assumed away.

**What it does not mean.** The architecture is not novel. GINE, Deep Sets and
temperature scaling are all published methods used as published. Our contribution
is the control design, not the network.

---

## 6. Hyperparameter selection

**What we did.** Ran 32 configurations at 3 seeds each — 96 runs — chose the
winner by validation AUPRC, froze it, and only then opened the test set.

**Why.** Choosing anything by looking at test results makes the test score
meaningless.

**What it means.** Our test number is an honest estimate.

**What it does not mean.** It does not mean our configuration is optimal. It
means it was the best of 32 on validation, and we committed to it.

*If asked "did you look at test more than once?"*: the test set was evaluated
once for the primary model. Ablations and controls were also evaluated on it,
which is why we applied Holm correction across the preregistered comparisons and
label the post-hoc analyses as post-hoc.

---

## 7. The main result

**What we did.** Compared BIO-GINE (0.8117) against an identical model with the
biological branch switched off (0.7784).

**What it means.** Adding biology improved performance by 0.0333 AUPRC, and all
five seeds moved the same direction, which is why the effect size is large
(dz = 7.72) even though the absolute gap is modest.

**What it does not mean.** 0.81 is not "81% accurate". AUPRC is the area under
the precision–recall curve; at 50% prevalence a random model scores 0.5. And the
number alone means nothing — it is only interpretable next to the baselines and
controls.

---

## 8. S3 — the hardest condition

**What we did.** Reported the 7,758 pairs where both drugs are unseen.

**Why.** This is where a model that leans on the known interaction graph has
literally nothing to work with.

**What it means.** BIO-GINE reached 0.7372 here while the model with a
DDI-network branch collapsed to 0.6198 and became four times more variable
between seeds. Biological information degraded gracefully; interaction-graph
context did not.

**What it does not mean.** S3 drugs are **not new drugs**. They are
well-documented drugs whose records we hid. A real new drug also has *sparse*
annotations, not just hidden ones. S3 performance is an optimistic bound on what
to expect for a genuinely novel compound.

---

## 9. The controls — the most important part

**What we did.** Two tests of the "it's just annotation popularity" explanation.

*CONTROL A:* a model given **only** annotation counts. It reached 0.6504.

*CONTROL F:* rewired which specific proteins each drug is annotated against,
while keeping the number of annotations per drug and per protein **exactly the
same**. Retrained from scratch. Performance fell to 0.6923.

**Why.** Well-studied drugs have more annotations *and* more documented
interactions. Without these controls, a biological gain could just be detecting
fame.

**What it means.** Counts alone get 0.6504 — so popularity genuinely is
predictive, and that is worth knowing. But identity matters much more: scrambling
which proteins go with which drug cost 0.12 AUPRC, more than the whole benefit of
adding biology in the first place.

**What it does not mean.** This does **not** show the model learned real
pharmacological mechanisms. The model could be relying on protein identity
because those proteins mark drug classes, or therapeutic areas, or annotation
habits. All of those depend on identity and none is a mechanism. Also, our
shuffle was imperfect: 2.53% of edges were left unchanged, and we did not shuffle
the protein-to-pathway layer.

---

## 10. The ablation ladder — where we report against ourselves

**What we did.** Added evidence sources one at a time: DrugBank relations (M1),
plus curated mechanism-of-action (M2), plus experimental bioactivity (M3), plus
Reactome pathways (M4).

**What it means.** Every version beat the no-biology baseline — the headline holds.
But the ladder goes **up then down**: M2 scores 0.8269 and M4, our primary model,
scores 0.8117. M4 is the *worst* of M1–M4.

**Why we still call M4 primary.** Because we committed to it on validation before
opening the test set. Switching to M2 after seeing test results is exactly the
behaviour that makes test scores meaningless.

**What it does not mean.** It does not mean pathways are useless — only that in
this study they did not help held-out performance. And our explanation (noisier
evidence, redundant pathway membership diluting a mean) is **post-hoc**. We did
not predict the ladder's shape in advance.

**Be ready for this one:** the SUM control beat our primary model too (0.8265 vs
0.8117). Our own preregistration said "if SUM wins, counting was the signal." The
honest answer is that both count and identity carry signal, and we traded away
some accuracy to make identity testable. Do not pretend the SUM result is not
there — a judge who finds it in the tables after you skipped it will trust
nothing else you said.

---

## 11. Calibration

**What we did.** Divided the model's outputs by a single number per seed, fitted
only on validation data.

**Why.** The raw model was very overconfident — it would say 95% when it should
say 70%.

**What it means.** Calibration error dropped roughly threefold. The probabilities
are now more meaningful as probabilities.

**What it does not mean.** It does **not** make the model more accurate. Dividing
by a constant cannot change the *order* of predictions, so AUPRC is unchanged (it
moved in the 7th decimal — floating-point noise). A calibrated probability from
this model is still a research prediction, not a clinical risk estimate.

---

## 12. Interpretability

**What we did.** Removed one annotation at a time from the frozen model and
measured how much the prediction moved.

**What it means.** The model relies most on protein annotations (mean absolute
change 0.313), then structure (0.192), then pathways (0.107). Reliance on
individual proteins is extremely concentrated: most single removals change
nothing at all, but one moved a prediction from 1.000 to 0.015.

**What it does not mean.** This is **model reliance**, not biology. Saying "this
protein causes the interaction" is not supportable. The right phrasing is:
*removing this annotation changed the model's prediction by X.* A protein the
model relies on might reflect real pharmacology, a correlate of it, or an
artefact of how databases are curated — and this study cannot distinguish those.

---

## 13. CONTROL E — the diagnostic that failed

**What we did.** Planned to test whether the learned biological embedding still
secretly encodes "how many interactions does this drug have".

**What happened.** It cannot be tested on held-out drugs. Under a strict
drug-disjoint split, *every* test drug has training-interaction degree exactly
zero. We verified this: minimum 0, maximum 0, variance 0.

**What it means.** R² is explained variance divided by total variance. With zero
total variance the answer is **undefined**, not zero. The `0.0` in our output file
is a placeholder, and the correlation fields are `NaN` — the fingerprint of a
constant target.

**What it does not mean.** It absolutely does **not** mean "R² = 0, therefore the
embedding does not encode degree." That would be claiming evidence from a
measurement that could not be made. On *training* drugs the R² is 0.543, so the
embedding does carry degree information there.

**How to say it:** "The planned diagnostic turned out to be non-identifiable —
our own split guarantees its target is constant on the test set. We report that
rather than the placeholder, and CONTROL F does the work instead."

This is a strong answer, not a weak one. Noticing and reporting a broken
diagnostic is better science than quietly reporting the zero.

---

## 14. H5 — the unsupported prediction

**What we did.** Predicted that biology would help more for pairs where both
drugs have pathway coverage.

**What happened.** The opposite direction, and not statistically distinguishable
from zero (Δ = −0.0233, p = 0.157).

**What it means.** The prediction was not supported.

**What it does not mean.** It is **not** a failed confirmatory hypothesis. It was
registered as *exploratory* from the start, with no significance threshold. The
correct phrase is "exploratory direction unsupported". No conclusion in the paper
depended on it. And the uncovered subgroup was small (5,527 pairs), so the test
had limited power — this is not evidence that pathways are useless either.

---

## 15. Limitations

**What we did.** Listed them at length, including several that weaken our own
story.

**Why.** A limitations section that only contains generic caveats signals that
the authors have not thought hard. The specific ones — non-monotonic ladder, SUM
beating the primary model, CONTROL E broken, scaffold-disjoint not run, 2.53%
shuffle overlap — are the ones that show the work was done carefully.

**What it means.** Judges can check any of these against the frozen files.

**What it does not mean.** Listing a limitation does not neutralise it. The
scaffold gap is a real gap; F5 is genuinely not fully resolved.

---

## The three sentences to have ready

1. *"We tested whether biological information about a drug can replace the
   interaction-network context that disappears when a drug is new — and then we
   built controls to check that we weren't just detecting how well-studied each
   drug is."*

2. *"Scrambling which proteins each drug is annotated against, while keeping the
   exact same number of annotations, cost more performance than adding biology
   gained in the first place. That's our strongest evidence that identity, not
   popularity, is doing the work."*

3. *"Our primary model isn't the best-performing variant in our own ablation
   table, and one of our controls beat it. We froze that model on validation
   before opening the test set, so we report it as it is."*
